If someone wanted to move into your home, you'd say no. If someone asked to borrow money, you'd think it over for a long time.

But if someone invites you to a meeting with no agenda, sends you a string of unimportant messages, or adds you to a group chat you don't want to be in, you'll probably say yes.

Seneca found this strange two thousand years ago.

## We don't lack time, we waste it

Seneca wrote *On the Shortness of Life* for a friend named Paulinus. He begins by noting that most people complain that nature is stingy. The time we're given is too short and goes by too fast. Even the physician says, "Art is long, life is short." Seneca sees it the other way around:

> The time we are given is not short. We waste a great deal of it. Life is long enough, long enough to accomplish the most important things, if only we arrange it well… The life we receive is not short; we make it short. We are not short of days; we squander them.
>
> — Seneca, *On the Shortness of Life* 1

## We guard our property closely, and our time carelessly

Seneca's sharpest observation comes in chapter 3:

> No one is willing to let others occupy his land, and over a small dispute about a boundary, people will pick up stones and clubs. Yet they let others take over their lives, and even lead those people in with their own hands… People carefully guard their property from being wasted, but when it comes to wasting time, they are most extravagant with the one thing they ought to spend most sparingly.
>
> — Seneca, *On the Shortness of Life* 3

Then he asks a man of a hundred to do the math. How much of his life went to creditors? To quarrels with his family? To running around the city on errands? To pointless grief and polite small talk? Subtract all that, and "you will find that you have lived fewer years than you count."

Then he names the cause:

> You live as if you would live forever. You never remember how fragile you are, and you never notice how much time has already gone by. You squander time as though it could never run out.
>
> — Seneca, *On the Shortness of Life* 3

## Waiting is the greatest waste

In chapter 3, Seneca mentions how many people say, "When I'm fifty, I'll retire and take it easy." Who guarantees you'll live that long? By chapter 9, he puts it even more strongly:

> Putting things off is the greatest waste of life. It takes our days away one by one, and it robs us of the present by promising us the future. The greatest obstacle to truly living is waiting: it hangs on tomorrow and loses today.
>
> — Seneca, *On the Shortness of Life* 9

In his first letter to Lucilius, he says it even more briefly:

> Lay hold of to-day's task, and you will not need to depend so much upon to-morrow's. While we are postponing, life speeds by. Nothing, Lucilius, is ours, except time.
>
> — Seneca, *Letters to Lucilius* 1.2–3

## This isn't a pitch for "being more productive"

At this point it's easy to read Seneca as a time-management guru. But he doesn't want you to pack your schedule tighter. He wants the opposite.

The "busy people" Seneca criticizes are exactly the ones with full calendars, rushing around all day. In his view, the people who truly live their time are the ones willing to spend it on thinking, reading, and reflection, because only that time really belongs to them.

So the question here isn't "Are you using your time efficiently enough?" It's "How much of your time did you actually choose?"

## A Zen view

In Case 7 of *The Gateless Gate*, a monk who has just arrived at the monastery asks Zen Master Zhaozhou, "I've just come here. Please teach me." Zhaozhou asks:

> "Have you eaten your rice gruel yet?" The monk said, "I have." Zhaozhou said, "Then go wash your bowl."
>
> — *The Gateless Gate*, Case 7

"Have you had breakfast?" "Yes." "Then go wash your bowl."

Seneca says the greatest waste is waiting, waiting for some future day to start really living. Zhaozhou's answer is even simpler. The practice you're looking for isn't somewhere in the future. It's right here, in washing your bowl.

One reminds you not to save your life for the end. The other reminds you that your life is right here, in this moment.

## Try it today: count up your day

Seneca asked a hundred-year-old man to count up his whole life. You can start with one day.

1. **Tonight, look back**: Roughly what did you spend your time on today?
2. **Mark each item**: ○ means I chose it; × means I was pushed into it.
3. **Change just one ×**: Tomorrow, change one of them. For example, don't scroll your phone on your commute, or turn down an unnecessary meeting.

Don't try to change everything at once. Seneca never said every minute should become your own. He just wants you to know that those minutes were yours to begin with.
