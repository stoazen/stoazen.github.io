"Remember that you will die." In Latin, *memento mori*.

It sounds heavy. But in the notebooks of the Stoics, it reads more like a friend patting you on the shoulder: you're still here today, so don't waste it.

<!-- truncate -->

## An emperor's reminder to himself

Marcus Aurelius was a Roman emperor. He had more power and wealth than anyone else in the world at the time. Yet in his *Meditations* he kept reminding himself:

> Since it is possible that you may depart from life this very moment, regulate every act and thought accordingly.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 2.11

> Do not act as if you were going to live ten thousand years. Death hangs over you. While you live, while it is in your power, be good.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 4.17

Notice that the point of the second passage isn't "death hangs over you." It's the last line: **be good**. You think of death to bring your attention back to what you can do right now.

Epictetus also recommended practicing this every day:

> Let death and exile and every other thing which appears dreadful be daily before your eyes; but most of all death: and you will never think of anything mean nor will you desire anything extravagantly.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 21

## Why thinking about death helps you live better

The Stoics' reasons were practical:

1. **You see what matters.** If today were your last day, would that angry message still be worth sending? Would the thank-you you've been meaning to say still have to wait?
2. **You stop putting things off.** Seneca said we live as if we'll live forever, and that's why we leave what we really want to do for "later" (*On the Shortness of Life* 3).
3. **You want less.** Epictetus said that someone who often thinks of death won't desire anything too much. Most of what we fight so hard over looks small when you measure it against a whole life.

## "I have given it back"

The Stoics looked at loss the same way. Epictetus said:

> Never say about anything, "I have lost it," but say, "I have restored it."
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 11

What he meant is that everything we have is on loan. We're like travelers staying at an inn: take good care of the place while you're there, and hand it back when you leave.

Another passage of his is even more direct: "If you're fond of a clay jug, say, 'What I'm fond of is a clay jug.' Then if it breaks, you won't be so upset." (*Enchiridion* 3) He goes on to apply the same idea to the people in your family, and that part is hard to read.

Here's how we see it: these words aren't asking you to love less. They're reminding you that because we will lose things, the time we have together now is precious. If you're going through a loss right now, there's no need to rush to argue yourself out of grief with philosophy. The Stoics also accepted sadness as a human response. These passages are better read on ordinary days, not used as a standard to hold yourself to when it hurts the most.

## Memento mori is not pessimism, and it is not depression

This practice is very different from getting stuck in thoughts of death:

| | The Stoic practice of death | A state worth worrying about |
|---|---|---|
| After thinking about death | You return to what you want to do today | Nothing seems to matter |
| How it feels | Clearer, more grateful | Heavy, helpless |
| How often | A short reminder | You can't stop |

If you find yourself thinking about death often and feeling hopeless, please don't carry it alone. In Taiwan, you can call the **1925 Peace of Mind Line** (24 hours, free). Elsewhere, call your local crisis line (in the US, call or text **988**), or talk with someone you trust or a mental health professional.

## How Zen sees it

In Case 6 of the *Blue Cliff Record*, the Zen master Yunmen said to his students: "I won't ask you about the days before the fifteenth. About the days after the fifteenth, say a word." No one could answer, so he answered for them:

> Every day is a good day.
>
> — *Blue Cliff Record*, Case 6

This line is often used as a blessing: "may every day be a good day." But it doesn't mean every day goes smoothly. Good days, bad days, ordinary days: each one is only this one day, and it won't come again.

The Stoics use death to remind you: today could be your last, so live it well. Yunmen says: whatever today looks like, it's the only day you have. One looks back from the finish line. The other simply stands in today.

## Try it today: one question before bed

You don't need to imagine your death every day. Try this gentle version instead.

Before bed, ask yourself one question: **If today were my last day, is there anything I'd wish I had done, or hadn't done?**

If there is, do that one thing tomorrow. It can be small: make a phone call, say thank you, leave work a little early and go home for dinner.
