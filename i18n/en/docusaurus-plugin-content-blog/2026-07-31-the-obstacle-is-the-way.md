Marcus Aurelius ruled for nearly twenty years, and he spent most of that time at war. At the same time, the empire was hit by a plague that dragged on for years, and the frontiers were under constant threat.

It was in the middle of all this that he wrote a line in his notebook that would be quoted again and again: the obstacle on the road helps us along the road.

<!-- truncate -->

## What the original says

The line comes from Book 5, section 20 of the *Meditations*. He was writing about something very ordinary: sometimes people get in the way of what you're supposed to do.

> …they may be impediments to my acts; but they are no impediments to my affects and disposition, which have the power of acting conditionally and changing: for the mind converts and changes every hindrance to its activity into an aid; and so that which is a hindrance is made a furtherance to an act; and that which is an obstacle on the road helps us on this road.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 5.20

The key is the difference between your *actions* and your *disposition*. Other people can make your plans fail. They can't decide what attitude you bring in response. When Plan A gets blocked, you still get to choose: patience, creativity, another route. The obstacle doesn't disappear, but it becomes the material for practicing those qualities.

## A shipwreck story

Zeno of Citium, the founder of Stoicism, was pushed onto this path by an obstacle. He started out as a merchant. A shipwreck cost him all his cargo and left him stranded in Athens, where he came across philosophy in a bookshop. He is said to have later remarked:

> The voyage on which I was shipwrecked turned out to be a good voyage.
>
> — Zeno, in Diogenes Laërtius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers* 7.4

Ancient sources tell this story in several versions, so all we can say is "he is said to have." But the line captures a Stoic way of seeing. Something that looks like failure right now may, on a longer timescale, turn out to be a turning point.

## Not "this is bad luck," but "bearing it well is good luck"

In Book 4 of the *Meditations*, Marcus is talking to himself:

> Be like the promontory against which the waves continually break, but it stands firm and tames the fury of the water around it.
>
> Unhappy am I, because this has happened to me.—Not so, but Happy am I, though this has happened to me, because I continue free from pain, neither crushed by the present nor fearing the future.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 4.49

He sums up the passage with a principle to remember whenever something troubles you: "this is not a misfortune, but that to bear it nobly is good fortune."

## How this idea gets misused

Today, "every setback is an opportunity" can easily become an empty pep talk, or even a source of pressure. As if, whenever something bad happens, you're obligated to turn it into a growth story.

What the Stoics meant was much plainer:

- **You don't have to pretend bad things are good.** Marcus never said the plague was a good thing. What he said was this: how you respond to it, and with what kind of character, is always in your hands.
- **Not every obstacle has to be flipped.** He also wrote that if something truly can't be done, don't grieve over it, because the reason it can't be done doesn't lie with you (*Meditations* 8.47). Sometimes "turning it into help" means accepting that this road is closed and putting your energy somewhere else.
- **You don't have to understand it now.** The meaning of Zeno's shipwreck only became clear many years later. For now, it's enough just to get through it.

## A Zen view

In Case 46 of *The Gateless Gate*, Master Shishuang asks a question:

> At the top of a hundred-foot pole, how do you take one step forward?
>
> — *The Gateless Gate*, Case 46

You have already climbed to the top of a hundred-foot pole. There is nowhere left to go. How do you take one more step forward?

The Stoics treat an obstacle as material, and build character right where they stand. Zen goes further. The place where the road ends is exactly where you are asked to let go and step forward. *The Gateless Gate* quotes an old master: "At the top of a hundred-foot pole you must step forward, and your whole body will appear throughout the ten directions."

One teaches you to stand firm in hardship. The other teaches you to let go when there's nowhere left to stand. Neither treats "no way forward" as the end.

## Try it today: three questions

Pick something that has you stuck right now and ask yourself:

1. **Which of my actions is this blocking?** Be specific: "This project got delayed. I wanted to finish it before the end of the year."
2. **What can't it block?** Your attitude, your patience, what you can learn from it.
3. **Which quality can I practice with it?** Patience, courage, honesty, self-control. Pick one.

If you can't answer any of the three, that's okay too. Today, just get through it. That is a practice as well.
