In the first century AD, a Roman was waiting to be tried by the Senate. Someone ran in to tell him the trial had begun.

He said, "I hope it goes well. But it's the fifth hour now. Let's go get some exercise." That was the time of day when he exercised and took a cold bath.

After his exercise, someone came again: "You've been condemned."

<!-- truncate -->

## Dinner after a trial

The man's name was Agrippinus, and the story is in Book 1, Chapter 1 of Epictetus's *Discourses*. After hearing the verdict, he asked only a few questions:

> "To banishment or to death?" "To banishment." "What about my property?" "It is not taken from you." "Let us go to Aricia then, and dine."
>
> — Epictetus, *Discourses* 1.1

This isn't coldness, and it isn't bravado. The verdict was not in his hands. Exercising, eating, and the frame of mind he set out with were. He put all his energy into the second group.

This is Stoicism's most essential tool. Later readers named it the "dichotomy of control."

## What the original says

It is the very first line of Epictetus's *Enchiridion*:

> Of things some are in our power, and others are not. In our power are opinion, movement towards a thing, desire, aversion; and in a word, whatever are our own acts: not in our power are the body, property, reputation, offices, and in a word, whatever are not our own acts.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 1

He goes on to say that if you treat what isn't yours as your own, "you will be hindered, you will lament, you will be disturbed, you will blame both gods and men."

Notice that "the body" is on the list. We usually assume our body is obviously ours. But whether you get sick, or how you age, you can only influence, not decide. Epictetus draws the line closer to the self than most people expect. The only things fully in your hands are how you see, how you choose, and how you respond.

## Three common misunderstandings

### Misunderstanding 1: "It's not in my hands, so I don't need to care"

The dichotomy of control isn't telling you to give up. Agrippinus didn't check out of his own life. He still exercised, and he still ate dinner. Your exam result isn't in your hands, but your preparation is. Whether someone accepts your apology isn't in your hands, but whether you apologize, and how, is.

What it does is **redistribute** your energy. Stop spending it on worrying about results, and put it back into what you can actually do.

### Misunderstanding 2: It's a kind of indifference

The Stoics took their responsibilities to others very seriously. Marcus Aurelius spent his entire life running an empire. The dichotomy of control keeps you from being held hostage by outcomes. It doesn't make you stop caring about people. You can love someone with your whole heart and still know that their choices are not in your hands.

### Misunderstanding 3: Once you see the line, it stops hurting

There is a long way between knowing and doing. Epictetus himself said these are things philosophers should think about, write down, and practice every day (*Discourses* 1.1). It's a practice, not a switch.

## "Not even Zeus can overpower my will"

In the same chapter, Epictetus writes an imaginary conversation with a tyrant who is threatening him:

> Chain me? You may fetter my leg, but my will not even Zeus himself can overpower.
>
> — Epictetus, *Discourses* 1.1

The man who said this had been a slave in his youth. His body really had once belonged to someone else. That's why he knew so clearly that there is one place no one else can enter.

## How Zen sees it

The Zen master Linji Yixuan said:

> Be your own master wherever you are, and wherever you stand is true.
>
> — *The Record of Linji*

Wherever you go, be the master of yourself. Wherever you stand, that place is real.

Zen doesn't split the world into two columns and analyze it. It's more direct: the place where you are standing right now is the place where you can act. Stoicism helps you see clearly which things you no longer need to carry around. Zen brings you back to the ground under your feet in this moment. One helps you let go; the other helps you stand firm.

## Try it today: the two-column list

Pick something that has had you stuck lately. Take a sheet of paper and draw two columns:

| Not in my hands | In my hands |
|---|---|
| Whether my manager likes my proposal | How thoroughly I prepare it |
| Whether they text me back | Whether to send another message, and what to say |
| Whether it rains tomorrow | Whether I bring an umbrella |

When you're done, just glance at the left column. Put all of today's energy into the first item on the right.

If the right column is empty, it means you've already done everything you can about this. So do what Agrippinus did: go exercise, go eat dinner.
