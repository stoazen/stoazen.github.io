More than 2,300 years ago, a merchant named Zeno was sailing from Phoenicia to Athens with a shipload of purple dye. Partway there, the ship sank.

The story goes that in Athens he walked into a bookshop, read a book about Socrates, and asked the bookseller where he could find men like that. Just then the philosopher Crates happened to walk by. The bookseller pointed at him and said: follow that man.

Years later, Zeno taught beside the Athenian marketplace, under a colonnade decorated with painted murals. That colonnade was called the Stoa, and his students came to be known as the Stoics.

## From a colonnade to a Roman emperor

Zeno's story is recorded in Book 7 of Diogenes Laertius's *Lives of the Eminent Philosophers*. The ancient sources tell the details in several versions, but one line is often quoted:

> The voyage on which I was shipwrecked turned out to be a good one.
>
> — Zeno, in Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of the Eminent Philosophers* 7.4

Stoicism was passed down in Greece for several hundred years and later spread to Rome. The complete works we can still read today come mainly from three people of the Roman era, and they could hardly have been more different:

| Person | Dates | Who they were | Key works |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seneca | c. 4 BC – AD 65 | Statesman and playwright; once tutor and adviser to the emperor Nero | *Letters to Lucilius* (*Moral Letters*), *On Anger*, *On the Shortness of Life* |
| Epictetus | c. 50–135 | Born a slave, later freed; ran a school in Nicopolis | The *Discourses* and the *Enchiridion* (*Handbook*), written down by his student Arrian |
| Marcus Aurelius | 121–180 | Roman emperor | *Meditations* |

A wealthy statesman, a teacher who had been a slave, and an emperor. Their lives looked nothing alike, yet they used the same method to take care of their minds. That's one reason Stoicism keeps being read: it works for anyone.

Marcus Aurelius never meant for the *Meditations* to be read by others. Several of its books end with a note of where they were written, such as "among the Quadi, on the Gran River" or "at Carnuntum." These were his camps while he was leading his armies. It is an emperor writing reminders to himself in a tent.

## Four core ideas

### 1. Know what is in your hands

The very first line of Epictetus's *Enchiridion* is:

> Of things some are in our power, and others are not.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 1

Your judgments, your choices, how you respond: these are in your hands. What other people think, whether your body gets sick, how things turn out: these are not fully in your hands. Most Stoic practice starts from this distinction. See "[The Dichotomy of Control](/blog/dichotomy-of-control)."

### 2. What hurts is often your judgment about things

> Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 5

Something happens, and a sentence instantly pops into your head: "He's out to get me." "Now it's all over." The Stoics ask you to leave a little space between that sentence and the facts. See "[It's Not Things That Upset You](/blog/its-not-things-but-judgments)."

### 3. Character is the only true good

The Stoics held that wealth, fame, and health are things you may reasonably *prefer*, but they are not truly good, because they can be taken away and can also be used to do harm. The only true good is character, traditionally divided into four virtues: **wisdom, justice, courage, and self-control**.

That sounds strict, but it brings a kind of freedom. If the only thing that really matters is how you live as a person, then when the world takes things from you, it can't take the thing that matters most.

### 4. Live according to nature, and work with others

When the Stoics talk about "living according to nature," they don't mean going back to the woods. They mean living the way a human being is meant to live: thinking clearly, and living alongside other people. Marcus Aurelius wrote that we are made for cooperation, "like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth" (*Meditations* 2.1).

## The most common misunderstanding: Stoicism is not suppressing emotion

In everyday English, "stoic" means "showing no feeling, enduring in silence." That's very far from Stoic philosophy itself.

What the Stoics wanted to deal with were the violent emotions that carry a person away: rage, panic, envy. And their method wasn't to grit their teeth. It was to go back and examine the judgment that set the emotion off. They also recognized good emotions, such as joy, caution, and rational wishes.

So Stoicism isn't "don't feel anything." It's "don't let an unexamined thought make your decisions for you."

## How Zen sees it

Stoicism and Zen come from completely different places. One grew up in Greece and Rome, through reasoning and writing. The other grew up in China, through koans, sitting meditation, and everyday work. Their basic views of the world also differ, and they shouldn't be described as the same thing.

But in practice, they often end up somewhere close. There is a famous Zen story: two monks were watching a temple flag flapping in the wind. One said the flag was moving. The other said the wind was moving. They argued on and on. Huineng, the Sixth Patriarch, said:

> It is not the wind that moves. It is not the flag that moves. It is your mind that moves.
>
> — *The Gateless Gate* (Wumenguan), Case 29

One takes judgments apart with reasoning. The other pulls you back to your own mind with a single sentence. StoaZen puts these two paths side by side because, when life gets hard, sometimes you need a clear argument, and sometimes what you need is one line that makes you stop.

## Where to start reading

- **Easiest way in**: Epictetus's *Enchiridion*. It's short; you can finish it in an afternoon.
- **Warmest**: Seneca's letters. It feels like a friend writing to you.
- **Most personal**: Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations*. You can read a passage or two each day.
