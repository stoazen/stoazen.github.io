An emperor, you'd think, would be the last person to worry about what others think.

But Marcus Aurelius wrote down a question in his notebook that even he couldn't figure out: why do we care so much about what other people think?

<!-- truncate -->

## Something even an emperor couldn't figure out

> I have often wondered how it is that every man loves himself more than all the rest of men, but yet sets less value on his own opinion of himself than on the opinion of others.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 12.4

He then ran a thought experiment. If a teacher told you that you had to say out loud whatever came into your mind, the moment it came, you couldn't stand it for a single day. This shows, he said, that "we have so much more respect to what our neighbours shall think of us than to what we shall think of ourselves."

Two thousand years later, the question has only gotten sharper. Likes, comments, messages left on "read": they're all other people's opinions, and now they're instant, public, and countable.

## Other people's opinions aren't in your hands

Look at it through the Stoic [dichotomy of control](/blog/dichotomy-of-control) and the answer is clear: what other people think belongs to the things that are "not up to us." In the very first section of the *Enchiridion*, Epictetus puts "reputation" in that column.

That doesn't mean other people's views don't matter. What the Stoics care about is dividing the work:

- **Other people's criticism**: you can listen to it and use it to check yourself.
- **How other people judge you**: not in your hands, and not something to decide who you are.
- **How you live**: entirely in your hands. This is the part you're responsible for.

## If you want to grow, be willing to look foolish

Epictetus gave people who wanted to improve a rather uncomfortable piece of advice:

> If you would improve, be content to be thought foolish and stupid with regard to externals.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 13

Starting anything new, like learning a skill, changing careers, or turning down an unreasonable request, can look clumsy or strange at first. If you need everyone to think well of you, it's very hard to begin.

## When someone talks about you behind your back

Epictetus left a very funny passage in the *Enchiridion*:

> If a man has reported to you that a certain person speaks ill of you, do not make any defence to what has been told you: but reply, "The man did not know the rest of my faults, for he would not have mentioned these only."
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 33

What makes this so clever is that it does three things at once: you don't take the bait, you don't rush to defend yourself, and you admit you were never perfect to begin with. Once you no longer need to prove you have no flaws, it doesn't hurt much when someone points them out.

In another section he reminds us that what makes you feel insulted isn't the person insulting you. It's your own judgment that "this is an insult" (*Enchiridion* 20). For more, see [It's Not Things That Upset You](/blog/its-not-things-but-judgments).

## Listen to criticism; don't carry judgments

The Stoics weren't telling people to stop listening to feedback. Here's a simple way to tell the two apart:

| | Criticism worth hearing | Judgments you don't need to carry |
|---|---|---|
| Content | Specific: "This report doesn't list its data sources" | Vague: "You're just not careful enough" |
| Aimed at | What you did | Who you are |
| What you can do | Fix it | Only let it affect your mood |

The first is a gift. The second is noise. Say thank you for the first. For the second, just notice it's there.

## How Zen sees it

In Case 12 of *The Gateless Gate*, the monk Ruiyan would call out to himself every day:

> "Master!" And he would answer himself, "Yes?" "Stay awake!" "Yes." "From now on, don't let anyone fool you!" "Yes, yes."
>
> — *The Gateless Gate* (Wumenguan), Case 12

Marcus Aurelius noticed that we take other people's views too seriously and our own too lightly. Ruiyan's approach was direct: every day, call yourself back and check whether the one in charge is still home.

But when Master Wumen commented on this case, he added a warning: "If you imitate him, it's all the view of a wild fox." Calling out to yourself every day just like Ruiyan is only a hollow copy. The point isn't the calling out. It's the wakefulness.

## Try it today: ask "Who wants this?"

Next time you're unsure whether to do something, like posting something, changing jobs, or turning down an invitation, first ask yourself:

1. **Do I want to do this because I think it's right, or because I'm afraid of what others will think?**
2. **If no one would ever know, would I still choose this?**

The answer doesn't have to be "I don't care about anyone else at all." But once you can see which part is what you want and which part is other people's voices, you've taken back your place as the one in charge.
