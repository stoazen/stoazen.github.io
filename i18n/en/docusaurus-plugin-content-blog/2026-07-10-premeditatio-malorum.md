In the first century AD, a great fire destroyed the city of Lugdunum in Gaul, today's Lyon. In a single night, nothing was left.

Seneca's friend Liberalis came from that city, and the news devastated him. Seneca wrote about it in a letter to another friend, Lucilius. The letter is about the fire, and also about how we should prepare for things like it.

<!-- truncate -->

## Not what usually happens, but what can happen

In the letter, Seneca says the hardest things to bear are the ones we didn't see coming. So:

> Nothing ought to be unexpected by us. Our minds should be sent forward in advance to meet all problems, and we should consider, not what is wont to happen, but what can happen.
>
> — Seneca, *Letters to Lucilius* 91.4

This practice later came to be called *praemeditatio malorum*, Latin for "the premeditation of evils." You imagine the bad things that could happen ahead of time, so that if they do arrive, they don't knock you flat.

Epictetus's version is more direct:

> Let death and exile and every other thing which appears dreadful be daily before your eyes; but most of all death: and you will never think of anything mean nor will you desire anything extravagantly.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 21

## But Seneca also said: don't suffer in your imagination

At this point you might be thinking: isn't this just telling people to worry more?

Interestingly, the same Seneca wrote almost the opposite in another letter:

> There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality.
>
> — Seneca, *Letters to Lucilius* 13.4

These two statements don't contradict each other. The key is **how you imagine**.

| | Imagining hardship in advance | Anxious rumination |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Prepare, then let go | No purpose, can't stop |
| After picturing something bad | Ask: if it really happens, what can I do? | Picture more bad things |
| Time | Has a beginning and an end | Replays endlessly |
| Result | Less afraid | More afraid |

Imagining hardship in advance is one clear rehearsal. You think it through once, plan your response, and come back to today. Anxiety is the same horror movie playing over and over, without ever getting an ending.

## Why imagining ahead helps

The Stoics had three reasons:

1. **It softens the blow.** When something you've already pictured actually happens, you're less likely to think, "How could this happen?" Seneca says the unexpected weighs on us most.
2. **It makes you value what you have now.** Once you've pictured losing something, you stop taking it for granted. Epictetus says this way you won't "desire anything extravagantly."
3. **It shows you what you can do right now.** After imagining a mishap, you may realize you could start an emergency fund, or say something you've been putting off.

## A Zen view

In Case 41 of *The Gateless Gate*, Huike goes to Bodhidharma and says, "My mind is not at peace. Master, please put it at peace for me." Bodhidharma says, "Bring me your mind, and I'll put it at peace for you." Huike goes off to look for it, then comes back and says:

> I have searched for the mind, and I cannot find it.
>
> — *The Gateless Gate*, Case 41

Bodhidharma says, "There. I have put it at peace for you."

Seneca says we suffer more in imagination than in reality. Bodhidharma goes a step further. He tells you to go and find out where that restless mind actually is. When you really look, it is often not as big as you thought.

Stoic premeditation puts your fear on the table and takes a good look at it. The Zen question looks clearly at what the fear itself is. Neither one runs away. Both look straight at it.

## Try it today: a ten-minute rehearsal

Pick one thing you're worried about. Give yourself ten minutes and write three lines:

1. **What's the worst that could happen?** Be specific. Don't write "Everything is ruined." Write something like "I don't get this job."
2. **If it really happens, what will I do?** "Apply to three more places, and ask a friend to do a mock interview with me."
3. **What can I do today?** "Revise my résumé one more time."

When the ten minutes are up, stop. Once you've written it down, hand the matter over to line three. Don't go back and replay it.

If you find you can't stop, and worry is filling most of your time, you may need more than practice. Please consider talking with a professional therapist.
