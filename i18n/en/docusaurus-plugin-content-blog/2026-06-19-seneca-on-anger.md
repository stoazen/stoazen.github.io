The message is already typed. Every word is sharp. Your finger is hovering over "Send."

Two thousand years ago, someone worked out what to do in this exact moment: don't press it yet.

<!-- truncate -->

## A whole book about anger

Seneca wrote *On Anger* because his older brother, Novatus, asked him to. Novatus wanted to know how anger could be calmed. The result runs to three books, and it is one of the most complete works on anger to come down to us from the ancient world.

He opens by quoting earlier thinkers on how dangerous anger is:

> Some of the wisest of men have in consequence called anger a short madness: for it is equally devoid of self control, regardless of decorum, forgetful of kinship, obstinately engrossed in whatever it begins to do, deaf to reason and advice.
>
> — Seneca, *On Anger* 1.1

He also says an angry person is like a falling rock. It smashes whatever it lands on, and it breaks apart along with it.

## The best method: delay

In Book 2, Seneca gives his most famous advice:

> The best cure for anger is delay. At first, ask your anger to grant you just this much time. Not so that you can forgive, but so that you can judge the matter properly. Delay it, and the anger will end.
>
> — Seneca, *On Anger* 2.29

Two points in this passage often get missed:

- **Delay is not the same as forgiveness.** Seneca isn't asking you to swallow it. He wants you to see the situation clearly first, and then decide what to do.
- **Don't try to crush it all at once.** He goes on to say that anger is fiercest when it first flares up. Rather than trying to put it out in one go, take it apart piece by piece.

He also points out that many of the things that make us angry reach us secondhand, and secondhand reports are often unreliable. Some people lie to stir up trouble. Others have been fooled themselves. So be "slow to believe what you are told."

Epictetus gives almost the same advice:

> If you once gain time and delay, you will more easily master yourself.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 20

## Why delay works

For the Stoics, anger isn't an uncontrollable storm. It is the result of a chain of judgments: "He hurt me." "He did it on purpose." "He deserves to be punished."

Delay gives you a chance to check every link in that chain. Did he really do it on purpose? Is this really that serious? Do I want to solve the problem, or do I just want to make the other person suffer?

Seneca also mentions a curious trick. Some angry people have looked in a mirror and been shocked by what they saw. But, he adds, what is truly ugly isn't the face. It's the mind twisted by anger, and no mirror can show you that.

## Marcus Aurelius: the best revenge

What if the other person really is in the wrong? Marcus Aurelius answers in a single line:

> The best way of avenging thyself is not to become like the wrong doer.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 6.6

Every morning, he also gave himself a kind of vaccine in advance:

> Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial. All these things happen to them by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 2.1

This isn't pessimism. It's a way of not being caught off guard. If you already know you'll run into these people today, you're less likely to take it as a personal attack when you do.

## At night, put the day on trial

Near the end of Book 3, Seneca describes a habit he practiced every day, which he learned from a philosopher named Sextius. Each night before sleep, ask yourself: Which bad habit did I cure today? In what way did I become better?

Seneca says anger calms down a great deal once it knows it will have to stand before the judge every night. In his own review he even tells himself, "You were too heated in that argument. From now on, don't debate with people who don't want to learn." You can find the full practice in [A Stoic Day](/blog/a-stoic-day).

## A Zen view

The *Song of Enlightenment* by Yongjia Xuanjue has this line:

> Let them slander, let them find fault. Trying to set the sky on fire with a torch, you only wear yourself out.
>
> — Yongjia Xuanjue, *Song of Enlightenment* (*Zhengdao Ge*)

Let people slander you and criticize you. It's like holding a torch up to burn the sky. You'll never reach the sky. You'll only exhaust yourself.

Seneca needed three books to analyze anger. Zen needs only one image: anger is a torch held up to the sky. Both point to the same truth. The first person anger burns is the one holding the fire.

## Try it today: three ways to delay

The next time your temper rises, pick one:

1. **Save the message as a draft.** Write it, but don't send it. Look at it again later. You'll probably delete half of it.
2. **Ask one question before you decide.** For example, "Did you mean that…?" A lot of anger comes from misunderstandings.
3. **Write a note at night.** Why did I get angry today? What was my judgment in the moment? Looking back now, do I still agree with it?

If your anger often gets out of control, or hurts you or the people around you, please talk with a professional therapist or doctor. Stoic philosophy can be a daily practice, but it is no substitute for professional help.
