The Stoics didn't see philosophy as a subject to study. They saw it as a way of life. Epictetus said these ideas are what philosophers "ought to think about, write down every day, and put into practice."

So this post skips the theory. It looks at four moments in a day, and at what the people in the original texts did in those moments.

## When you can't get out of bed

Marcus Aurelius had trouble getting up, too. At the start of Book 5 of the *Meditations*, he argues with himself:

> In the morning when you rise unwillingly, let this thought be present: I am rising to the work of a human being.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 5.1

Then he pushes back on himself: Was I made to lie under the blankets and keep warm? "But this is more pleasant." So you were born for pleasure, and not for doing things? He even points to little plants, sparrows, ants, spiders, and bees: each of them doing its work in its own place.

And then he admits: "But we need rest, too." Yes, we do. It's just that rest has its limits.

**Practice**: When you get up, tell yourself in one sentence what "the work of a human being" is for you today. It doesn't have to be grand. "Finish that report" or "really be there with my kids at dinner" both count.

## Before you head out: picture the people you'll meet

> Begin the morning by saying to yourself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial. All these things happen to them by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 2.1

This isn't cursing other people. It's giving yourself a vaccine. If you already know you'll meet difficult people today, it won't be such a surprise when you do, and you'll be less likely to take their behavior personally.

In the second half of the same passage he says we are made for cooperation, like hands, like feet, like the upper and lower rows of teeth. So after you've pictured the day, remember to put the other person back where they belong: as a fellow human being on your side.

**Practice**: Before you leave, take one minute. Think of the one person or thing most likely to upset you today, and how you'd like to respond. For a fuller version, see [Imagining Hardship in Advance](/blog/premeditatio-malorum).

## During the day: retreat into yourself

> Men seek retreats for themselves, houses in the country, sea-shores, and mountains ... but it is in your power whenever you choose to retire into yourself. For nowhere either with more quiet or more freedom from trouble does a man retire than into his own soul.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 4.3

An emperor leading armies in the field couldn't just drop everything and go on vacation. The rest he found didn't need a place. It only needed a few minutes of coming back to himself.

He also suggests keeping a few "brief and fundamental" principles on hand, ones that calm your mind the moment you recall them. Seneca says something similar in one of his letters:

> You need a change of soul rather than a change of climate.
>
> — Seneca, *Letters to Lucilius* 28.1

**Practice**: Find three gaps in your day, like waiting for the elevator, waiting for the water to boil, or the moment before a meeting starts. Stop and take three breaths. You don't need to think about anything. Just come back to yourself.

## When you're irritated: look down from above

> Look down from above on the countless herds of men and their countless solemnities, and the infinitely varied voyagings in storms and calms, and the differences among those who are born, who live together, and die.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 9.30

This practice later came to be called "the view from above." Imagine a camera pulling up from the room you're in: the whole building, the whole city, the whole island, the whole planet. How big is the thing that's bothering you now, in that picture?

This doesn't mean your worries don't matter. It helps you find a sense of proportion. A lot of what gets us stuck, gets us stuck because we're standing too close.

**Practice**: When you're irritated, close your eyes, pull the camera up three levels, then come back and look at the problem again.

## Before sleep: put the day on trial

This is one of the best-known daily Stoic practices. It's recorded in Seneca's *On Anger*:

> Sextius had this habit: at the end of each day, when he had lain down to rest, he would question his mind: "What bad habit did you cure today? What vice did you resist? In what way are you better?"
>
> — Seneca, *On Anger* 3.36

Seneca says he does the same thing himself:

> Once the lamp has been taken away, and my wife, who knows my habit, has fallen silent, I examine my whole day and go back over everything I said and did. I hide nothing from myself, and I skip nothing.
>
> — Seneca, *On Anger* 3.36

What matters most is the tone he takes in his review. What he says to himself is: "This time I forgive you. Don't do it again." It isn't self-blame. It's more like a kind but honest teacher. After an examination like this, he says, his sleep is especially peaceful.

**Practice**: Before bed, spend three minutes answering three questions:

1. What did I do well today?
2. What could I have done better?
3. What do I want to do tomorrow?

Then tell yourself: "That's it for today."

## How Zen sees it

In Case 19 of *The Gateless Gate*, Zhaozhou asked the Zen master Nanquan: What is the Way? Nanquan said:

> Ordinary mind is the Way.
>
> — *The Gateless Gate* (Wumenguan), Case 19

Zhaozhou asked again: Then can I work toward it? Nanquan said: "The moment you aim at it, you miss it." As soon as you try to head toward it, you've gone off course.

This is a good reminder for the Stoic daily practices. They can easily turn into one more to-do list: Did I do my morning preparation today? My evening review? Miss one day and you feel like you've failed.

What Nanquan is saying is that practice doesn't live in some special action. It lives in every ordinary moment. The Stoics give you reminders for four moments of the day. Zen reminds you not to turn the reminders themselves into a new source of pressure. If you miss a day, just start again tomorrow.
