Your manager replies in the team chat: "Take another look at this."

Within five seconds, a whole drama has played out in your head. He's not happy. He thinks I'm not good enough. My performance review is done for. Maybe I should start looking for another job.

The facts amount to one sentence. Everything else, you added.

## Stoicism's most quoted line

> Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things: for example, death is nothing terrible, for if it were, it would have seemed so to Socrates; for the opinion about death, that it is terrible, is the terrible thing.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 5

Epictetus chose an extreme example: death. Socrates was calm when he was sentenced to death, so death itself doesn't necessarily bring fear. What brings fear is the judgment we make about death.

A few decades later, Marcus Aurelius wrote the same idea even more briefly in the *Meditations*:

> Take away thy opinion, and then there is taken away the complaint, "I have been harmed." Take away the complaint, "I have been harmed," and the harm is taken away.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 4.7

## Event, impression, judgment: there's a gap in between

The Stoics broke down the way something gets into your mind into a few steps:

1. **Something happens**: your manager replies, "Take another look at this."
2. **An impression appears**: your mind automatically produces, "He's not happy."
3. **You agree or don't agree with that impression**: only this step is your judgment.

The first two steps are fast and almost beyond your control. The Stoics don't ask you to stop thoughts from popping up. They ask you to pause before step three and ask: is this impression a fact, or my interpretation?

When Epictetus talks about being insulted, he gives some very practical advice:

> Remember that it is not he who reviles you or strikes you, who insults you, but it is your opinion about these things as being insulting. … Therefore especially try not to be carried away by the appearance. For if you once gain time and delay, you will more easily master yourself.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 20

"Gain time" is that gap. The wider the gap, the more chance you have to choose.

## What it isn't

This line is often misused to mean "you're just overthinking it." That's wrong.

- **It doesn't deny that things really happen.** Losing a job, getting sick, losing someone you love: these are real. The Stoics never said they don't exist.
- **It isn't telling you to blame yourself.** Epictetus says the uneducated person blames others, the person who has begun to learn blames himself, and the person whose education is complete blames no one (*Enchiridion* 5). The goal isn't to turn the blame on yourself. It's to stop looking for someone to blame.
- **It doesn't mean you can't be sad.** The Stoics distinguished between a "first reaction" and the "judgment that follows." Being startled, feeling your eyes sting: those are human reactions. Whether you go on to conclude "my life is ruined" is the part you can examine.

Marcus Aurelius put it very clearly:

> If thou art pained by any external thing, it is not this thing that disturbs thee, but thy own judgement about it. And it is in thy power to wipe out this judgement now.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 8.47

Notice that he says "in thy power," not "easy."

## How Zen sees it

Case 29 of *The Gateless Gate* (Wumenguan) is the famous story of the wind and the flag. The wind was blowing the flag in front of a temple, and two monks started arguing. One said the flag was moving; the other said the wind was moving. Huineng, the Sixth Patriarch, said:

> It is not the wind that moves. It is not the flag that moves. It is your mind that moves.
>
> — *The Gateless Gate* (Wumenguan), Case 29

The Stoics patiently separate "the thing" from "the judgment" and check them step by step. Zen doesn't debate you about the wind and the flag. It turns your gaze straight back around: what's moving is your mind.

Both point to the same place. One uses analysis; the other uses a single sentence to make you stop.

## Try it today: write the facts and the story separately

The next time something makes your heart sink, open the notes app on your phone and write two lines:

- **Facts**: only what a camera could record. "My manager replied: Take another look at this."
- **Story**: the interpretation your mind automatically came up with. "He's not happy. He thinks I'm not good enough."

Then ask yourself one question: could the story line be something else? Maybe he's just busy. Maybe he's pointing out a detail for you.

You don't have to believe the kinder version right away. Once you see that there's more than one possible story, the gap opens.
